How It Works
Condition: Obese / High BMI

Organ pairs with mean Otsuka–Ochiai coefficient ≥ 0.01938 in the obese / high bmi literature context.

Per-Organ Query
(MeSH_ORGAN OR aliases_ORGAN) AND METABOLIC_FILTER AND CONDITION_FILTER (Obese / High BMI)

In each bootstrap iteration 10% of each organ's paper pool is sampled and cross-organ mentions are counted. A paper only counts as a cross-mention if the two organs' names (or aliases) appear together in the same sentence of its title/abstract. The resulting mean count is then normalized into an Otsuka–Ochiai coefficient:

OO(A, B) = mean cross-mentions √(nA × nB)

An organ with a much larger literature pool doesn't dominate edges just by volume. Edges reflect how specifically two organs are tied together in the literature.

Key Player Scores

Each edge shows metabolites, hormones, and proteins with their bootstrap frequency which is the fraction of the 50 iterations in which that molecule was mentioned in the sampled co-occurring papers. A score of 90% means the molecule appeared in 9 out of 10 random samples, indicating consistent literature support.

How papers are selected

One PubMed query is run per organ, ANDing the organ terms with the metabolic filter, the condition filter, and the crosstalk filter below. Up to 50,000 papers are fetched per organ. During bootstrapping, papers from one organ's pool that mention another organ in their title/abstract are counted as cross-organ evidence. Both organs have to appear in the same sentence. As normalization the Otsuka–Ochiai coefficient is used so a heavily studied organ's total literature pool size (n_found) doesn't dominate purely by volume.

Organ and crosstalk terms are matched as whole words, not substrings — "renal" no longer matches inside "adrenal", so a paper about the adrenal gland can't be mistaken for kidney evidence. A match is also discarded if it's negated — "non-renal", "nonrenal", "non renal", and "not renal" don't count as a positive mention of that organ or crosstalk term.

METABOLIC_FILTER

At least one of these keywords must appear in title or abstract. Ensures every retrieved paper is about metabolism, not just anatomy.

metabolism metabolic catabolism anabolism biosynthesis metabolite flux substrate nutrient fuel glucose glucose uptake glycolysis gluconeogenesis energy bioenergetics mitochondria mitochondrial thermogenesis Krebs cycle electron transport oxidation
CONDITION_FILTER — Obese / High BMI

At least one of these keywords must also appear, restricting results to the obese / high bmi physiological context.

obesity obese subjects obese patients obese volunteers obese adults adiposity patients with adiposity
CROSSTALK_FILTER

At least one of these keywords must also appear somewhere in the document (the PubMed query's coarse pre-filter) — restricting results to papers actually discussing inter-organ relationships, not just papers that happen to mention an organ and a metabolic term without relating them to anything else. After fetching, papers are filtered further: kept only if the organ's own name/alias and one of these crosstalk terms appear together in the same sentence of the title/abstract, not merely somewhere in the same document.

LLM Classification

For each robust organ pair an LLM (llama3.2, via Ollama) reads up to 100 of this pair's same-sentence cross-mention papers (the same bootstrap evidence used for the AI summary) and assigns 1 to 3 connection types from the categories below, ranked from most to least feasible — a pair only gets a second or third type if it is independently well-supported by the papers.

To reduce single-sample noise, the classification is run 3 times independently per pair; only types that appear in at least 2 of the 3 runs are kept (majority vote). If the 3 runs disagree on everything, the single most-voted type is kept, so every classified pair always ends up with at least one type.

Generated by run_llm_descriptions.py — run it (or add --reset to reclassify after editing CONNECTION_TYPES in this condition's config) then rebuild with --viz-only.

Connection Type Categories
Direct glucose transferOne organ releases glucose into circulation that the other directly takes up as primary fuel.
Hormonal glucose controlOne organ secretes a hormone (insulin, glucagon, GLP-1, GIP) that directly regulates glucose handling in the other.
Hormonal influenceOne organ sectretes a hormone that influences the metabolism of funciton of another organ while not directly affecting glucose handling.
Nerve connectionThe interaction is via an actual physical nerve connection (e.g. vagus nerve in the gut-brain-axis).
Other physical connectionNon nerve but still physical connection Could e.g. be mechanical influence of one organ on the other
Ketone body exchangeKetone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate) produced by hepatic ketogenesis are exported and oxidized as fuel by the other organ, especially during fasting or low-carb states.
Mineral/electrolyte-coupled metabolic regulationOne organ regulates a mineral or electrolyte (calcium, phosphate, iron) whose circulating level feeds back on metabolic enzyme activity or energy handling in the other.
Adipokine/myokine signalingOne organ secretes an adipokine or myokine (leptin, adiponectin, irisin, FGF21) that acts on metabolic pathways in the other, beyond core glucose-regulating hormones.
Inflammatory/cytokine crosstalkCytokines released by one organ drive inflammation that alters metabolism or function in the other.
Metabolite crosstalkMetabolites produced by one organact directly on the other organ to influence its metabolism.
Circadian/clock synchronizationThe molecular clock in one organ entrains or is entrained by the other's clock, and misalignment between them is linked to disrupted metabolic rhythms.

All assigned types are shown as coloured badges on each edge in the network.
